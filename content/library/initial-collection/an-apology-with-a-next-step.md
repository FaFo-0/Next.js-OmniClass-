# An Apology with a Next Step

During a team lunch, Tom repeats something Salma told him privately: she is considering applying for another job. She changes the subject, but later asks to speak to him alone.

Salma: I want to talk about what you said at lunch. I hadn't told the team I was looking elsewhere. Now my manager may hear about it before I've even decided to apply.

Tom: I'm sorry. I thought you were just discussing possibilities. I didn't realise it was confidential.

Salma: I understand that I didn't use the word "confidential". But it was a conversation between us, and I didn't give you permission to pass it on.

Tom: You're right. I should have asked. I put you in a difficult position.

Salma: Yes. And when you laughed about me "escaping", it made it sound as if I dislike the team. That isn't what I said.

Tom: I was trying to make a joke, but I can see how it sounded. Would it help if I sent everyone a message explaining that?

Salma: Please don't send anything yet. A group message might draw more attention to it. I need time to think about what I want my manager to know.

Tom: All right. What would you like me to do if someone asks about it?

Salma: Say that you shouldn't have spoken for me and that they should ask me directly. Don't add more details, even if you think they'll make things better.

Tom: I can do that. And I'll check with you before repeating anything personal in future.

Salma: Thank you. I'm still upset. I don't want you to take that as a sign that I haven't heard your apology.

Tom: I won't. You don't have to make me feel better about it.

The following day, a colleague asks Tom whether Salma has accepted another position. He gives the answer they agreed on. He doesn't tell Salma immediately and expect praise; he simply keeps the boundary.

For Salma, the apology matters, but the next few weeks matter more. Trust will depend on whether Tom's behaviour changes when there is no uncomfortable conversation reminding him to be careful.
