# A Better Way to Split the Bill

Six friends finish dinner. The food was good, the conversation was lively, and then the bill arrives. Someone suggests dividing it equally. One person goes quiet.

Equal shares are simple, but they aren't always comfortable. Perhaps one friend only had soup while another ordered three courses. Perhaps someone chose a cheap meal because they are saving for a journey. A small difference in cost can feel much larger when nobody has asked.

When Amir started a new job, his colleagues invited him to dinner. He checked the menu online and knew he could afford a main course. At the table, people ordered several shared dishes without discussing the price. He enjoyed the evening until the bill came. His share was almost twice what he had planned to spend.

He paid without saying anything. Later, he turned down two more invitations. His colleagues assumed he didn't enjoy spending time with them.

Eventually, a colleague asked him about it privately. Amir explained the problem. At the next dinner, she suggested that everyone pay for their own food and agree on shared dishes before ordering them. Nobody objected.

You don't need a complicated calculation for every meal. You do need an arrangement that people understand. Before ordering, try a neutral question: "Shall we each pay for what we have?" If everyone wants to share, ask what they would like to order and check the total together.

Restaurant staff may have limits on separate payments. Ask early rather than handing them six cards after they have printed one bill. A friend can pay the restaurant while the others send their agreed amounts, if everyone is comfortable with that.

The aim isn't to count every mouthful. It is to make sure the invitation feels like an invitation, rather than an unexpected expense.
