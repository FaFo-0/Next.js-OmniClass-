# The Hidden Work of Being Available

At 8:47 on a Sunday evening, Elena's phone lit up. Her manager had sent a short message: "Could you check the figures before tomorrow? No rush." Elena was watching a film with her brother. She put the phone face down, then picked it up again three minutes later.

The words said that the task could wait. The timing suggested something else. If she ignored it, would she seem unreliable? If she answered, would Sunday evening become a normal time to work?

Availability can look like a personal habit, but it is often a team agreement that nobody has actually made. One colleague sends messages whenever an idea occurs to him, intending others to read them later. Another interprets every notification as a request for immediate action. Both believe they are being considerate, yet one person's convenience creates another person's interruption.

In Elena's team, the problem became visible when a new employee asked which channel to use for urgent issues. There was no clear answer. Routine questions arrived in the same chat as genuine emergencies, and the most responsive person usually dealt with both.

The team tried a modest change. Ordinary messages could still be written at any time, but replies were expected during working hours. Anything needed earlier had to include a clear deadline. Problems that genuinely couldn't wait required a phone call, along with an explanation of why waiting would cause harm.

The arrangement wasn't perfect. The first week, Elena still checked her phone automatically. Her manager also had to change his behaviour: he scheduled non-urgent messages for the following morning instead of sending them late at night. Gradually, silence outside work stopped looking like a lack of commitment.

There are jobs where someone must be available after hours. In those cases, the useful questions are who is responsible, for how long, and how that responsibility is recognised. An unwritten expectation that everyone should always be reachable answers none of them.

A boundary is more convincing when it describes what will happen, rather than merely asking people to be respectful. "We value your free time" sounds reassuring. "Messages sent after six will normally receive a reply the next working day" gives people something they can act on.

Elena finished the film that Sunday. On Monday morning, she checked the figures. The work was completed, and her brother didn't have to compete with a spreadsheet for the rest of the evening.
